# Five concepts

All concepts are adults-first, optionally solo, usable from a normal phone screen and private by default. The world is a recognizable place to play, explore or plan. Live weather and local time alter opportunities, not pressure to go outside. Forecasts carry timestamps and uncertainty; unavailable data produces a neutral browse mode. None sells movement or home data.

## 1. Common Ground — a shared weekly walking game

**Core loop:** choose a broad area manually → select a short curated public-space challenge → walk or use an equivalent seated/browse version → record completion on device → optionally contribute one anonymous completion → unlock a shared seasonal scene at the next weekly release.

**Why it is fun:** a small reason to notice the familiar, a visible collective project and an attainable personal finish. Example: find three kinds of street trees from the public sidewalk. No speed ranking, streak loss or competitive territory capture. Social responses are preset encouragement; nobody sees who did what or where.

**Real world / weather / time:** real public paths and landmark shells frame a curated 10–20 minute loop. Clear afternoons suggest shade/architecture prompts; rain switches to an indoor map puzzle or another day; dusk selects a daylight alternative rather than treating a render as evidence of street safety. Engine sun and weather make the same street feel different. Curated route facts remain separate from visual weather. Accessibility alternatives count equally.

**Monetization hypothesis:** free weekly challenge, paid optional seasonal packs and private solo collections; licensed challenge packages for parks and neighbourhood organizations. A sponsor may fund a whole seasonal pack, clearly labeled, without targeting participants or accessing contributions. Never paywall privacy or equal participation.

**Risks:** repetition, false completion, uneven neighbourhood content, unsafe routing, mobility exclusion, and sparse aggregates. Accept self-report and modest cheating; curate paths, remove time pressure and use equivalent alternatives. A low-density area shows the illustration without activity counts. Weather freshness and accessibility gaps can invalidate a suggestion.

## 2. Gather — plan a small public-place gathering

**Core loop:** choose an eligible public plaza/park → start with a Builder template → place a few tents/tables/blank signs → compare time and weather → review access and permissions → save a private plan → preview an invitation with a public meeting place.

**Why it is fun:** a recognizable place becomes a shared possibility. People can vote between two plan options without sharing attendance or locations. Keep the prototype to a picnic/social afternoon; block-party street closures are a later professional workflow, not a one-tap promise.

**Real world / weather / time:** true-scale street/terrain context explains distances and arrangement. Weather compares rain/heat/wind scenarios; sun/shadow helps choose time. Forecast uncertainty remains visible. Public-space access, surface and permit facts need separate verification. Builder parts create the plan; rendered fit is not authorization or engineering validation.

**Monetization hypothesis:** free simple plan and saved preview; paid template sets and exports, organizer subscription, or organization licence. Charge for planning utility, not participant identity or attendance. No payment flow in the first prototype.

**Risks:** permit misunderstanding, blocked access, unsafe temporary structures, invitation leakage, low recurrence, organizer burden. A draft watermark persists until real approval is documented. Only vetted public sites; no residential party addresses. Use a small static object catalogue, open access corridor and explicit forecast fallback. No attendee map or roster; RSVPs are aggregate ranges only.

## 3. Season Together — cooperative seasonal neighbourhood puzzles

**Core loop:** open a seasonal chapter → solve a short puzzle about public architecture/landscape → optionally contribute a completion → watch a non-spatial shared progress scene → return next chapter.

**Why it is fun:** a familiar world changes over weeks, with cooperative reveals rather than winners. Chapters can be played at home without saying where home is.

**Real world / weather / time:** weather and daylight switch puzzle variants; autumn tree colour, winter shadow and spring rain become observations with an indoor equivalent. All tasks concern public places. Art depicts conditions, not predictions that a specific tree or surface is safe.

**Monetization hypothesis:** paid curated chapter packs and organization licensing; free foundational chapter. No chance-based purchases or monetized urgency.

**Risks:** seasonal content cost, thin social value, cultural mismatch and event fatigue. Test a single chapter before a full calendar. Never require snow, darkness or a storm to participate; redistribute progress only as broad delayed aggregates.

## 4. Nearby Notes — a public-place discovery collection

**Core loop:** choose an area and theme → preview a small curated collection → visit or explore it in 3D → save a private local stamp → suggest a correction through moderation.

**Why it is fun:** learn something small about an ordinary place and assemble a personal collection. Friends can share a collection template, not visit histories.

**Real world / weather / time:** façades, public art without copied protected artwork, civic places and landscape features form the map. Rain offers sheltered publicly accessible alternatives; opening hours and daylight control recommendations. Weather never certifies that a venue is open. Local discovery listings must be licensed or independently authored.

**Monetization hypothesis:** curated city packs, optional editorial subscription and explicitly labeled public-place partnerships. No behavioural advertising or uploaded personal visit history.

**Risks:** stale hours, licensed-content costs, misleading accessibility, accidental residential clues and biased commercial promotion. Use documented public sites, source dates, correction queues and non-commercial collections. Do not accept free-text residential pins.

## 5. Block Lab — playful public-space design challenges

**Core loop:** start with a public-space design brief → arrange limited Builder parts → compare daylight/weather scenarios → submit a plan stripped of identity → vote on broad design choices → see a delayed summary.

**Why it is fun:** playful constraints and tangible design tradeoffs; people respond to a plan rather than someone’s personal life. Example: fit shade, seating and a clear route into a civic plaza.

**Real world / weather / time:** real geometry exposes scale and shadow; rain/wind variants change the design brief. It is a game, not civic consultation or a claim that a plan is feasible. Only approved public-space templates.

**Monetization hypothesis:** organization-run challenge licences and optional object/template packs; private design sandbox remains useful even without a community.

**Risks:** confused public mandate, copying/IP, hostile voting, unrealistic engineering and moderation cost. Use constrained anonymous submissions, preset feedback, licensed parts and a clear conceptual label. No real resident profiles, home pins or public message feed.

## Selection judgment (hypothesis, not user research)

| Concept | Repeat use | Social value without surveillance | Builder dependency | Prototype burden | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Common Ground | Weekly | Cooperative project | Low | Medium | Lead experiment |
| Gather | Episodic | Shared public-place plan | High, useful after Builder | Medium-high | Second UX track, narrow pilot |
| Season Together | Seasonal | Cooperative chapter | Low | Medium content burden | Reuse as Common Ground content |
| Nearby Notes | Occasional | Share collections | Low | High editorial freshness | Later acquisition feature |
| Block Lab | Brief-based | Plan voting | High | High moderation burden | Later partner experiment |

Common Ground tests whether recognizable 3D place plus small shared goals changes repeat use. Gather tests whether Builder has a social planning payoff. Do not build five separate products or launch a general local social feed.
