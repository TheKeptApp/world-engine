# Apps on WorldEngine

Strategy brainstorm dated 2026-10-06. **80 scored concepts: 20 supplied concepts and 60 additions.** The supplied set includes DogWell, Neighborhood Jobs and all 18 ideas in your list. New ideas span consumer products, games, sports, B2B, licensing, education, health, accessibility, enterprise and unusual uses.

This uses your description of the engine as the starting point: true scale, stylized real places, live weather/sky/seasons/stars/satellites/transit/planes, a detailed map layer and 60 fps on iPhone, with web next. It does not independently verify performance or available datasets. Scores, target buyers and proposed prices are **strategy hypotheses**, not measured market size, proven demand or observed willingness to pay.

## Recommendation

**Sell one running-race preview pilot first (E04), while keeping DogWell's existing product work focused.** Sponsored neighborhood quests (N01) rank highest for upside and founder fit, but a fixed race course gives you a simpler brief, recognizable buyer and clearer delivery boundary. Build reusable route annotations, viewpoints and branded scenes that can later support a quest campaign give mor a tournament arrival guide. Do not start ten products at once.

Neighborhood Jobs ranks #11 rather than appearing in the top ten. Its local-world premise and sponsor fit score well; gameplay polish and a continuing content pipeline reduce ease compared with course/arrival tools. Treat it as an existing owned product to improve and validate, not a failed idea.

Three commercial lines could cover most of the leading options:

- **Routes and arrivals:** race briefing, coach briefing, tournament arrival, ordinary event arrival and game-day venue experience.
- **Partner programs:** sponsored neighborhood quests and charity challenges, with optional use of owned-game assets.
- **Scene outputs:** brand clips and scouting storyboards, using the same camera/viewpoint tools.

Keep the immediate build to one paid workflow. Shared code is not proof that different buyers will accept one package.

## Scoring method

Each idea has five integer scores from 1 to 5. Higher is better, including **ease**, where 5 means easiest.

| Criterion | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engine strengths | A basic map/list would mostly suffice | 3D or live context helps | Real scale, familiar places, sky/live layers or spatial interaction are central |
| Market size | Very narrow buyer/user pool | Meaningful specialist segment | Broad cross-market buyer/user pool |
| Ease of building | Major new systems/data plus difficult operations | Manageable additions with meaningful dependencies | Very small bounded workflow on capabilities already supplied |
| Willingness to pay | Weak paid reason or unclear payer | Plausible paid use, unvalidated | Concrete organizational deliverable or strong purchase hypothesis |
| Solo-founder fit | Specialist sales/build/support far from your background | Plausible but needs learning or partners | Sports/brand distribution fit and a bounded delivery model |

Market size means a **relative addressable audience judgment**, not dollar TAM. Willingness to pay means a **purchase hypothesis**, not that a budget or signed buyer exists. Fit combines your sports/brand background with solo selling and supporting capacity. Ease includes verification, content, data rights, integrations and ongoing support. A score of 4 is relative to this idea set, not a delivery-time guarantee.

The CSV contains both an unweighted sum out of 25 and a founder-oriented weighted score out of 100:

**Weighted = 5 × engine + 3 × market + 5 × ease + 3 × pay + 4 × founder fit.**

This corresponds to 25%, 15%, 25%, 15% and 20% respectively, normalized against a perfect 5/5 score. The minimum possible weighted score is 20; the maximum is 100. Sort by weighted score descending. Ties break by ease, founder fit, engine strength, then ID ascending. The top ten below follow that rule exactly; recommendation of the first pilot also considers commercial sequencing.

All ranks are a fixed snapshot in the CSV. If you edit scores, recompute the weighted sum and sort again using the same tie rule. One-point differences are judgments, not precision estimates; near-ties deserve customer validation.

The file includes each concept, buyer, test price, five scores, both sums, rank, top-ten flag, overlap group/IDs, ownership/licensing policy, narrow MVP, primary dependency and score rationale. IDs E01–E20 preserve the supplied set; N01–N60 identify additions. New concepts include adjacent workflows as well as new categories, and their overlap is explicitly shown rather than concealed.

### 1. Sponsored neighborhood quests (N01)

A brand buys a small neighborhood adventure that feels specific to the place: recognizable streets, local landmarks, the actual season and a handful of approved challenges. Start with a digital-only five-stop quest and a reward page, then add optional physical participation once verification is credible. Sell a campaign with a clear beginning, end and deliverable, rather than building a broad ad marketplace. Your advantage is combining the engine's sense of place with sports-brand relationships. Target buyer: sports brands and activation agencies. Pricing to test: $2,500 for one area, $10,000 for a defined multi-area package. Keep the consumer quest format and audience; license campaign use for a limited period.

### 2. Running race preview (E04)

Let a race entrant recognize the finish approach, rehearse turns and orient around unfamiliar landmarks before race morning. The director supplies the official route and corrections; WorldEngine turns them into a navigable course briefing that can carry approved sponsor assets. Start with a short route and a limited set of annotations, without live participant tracking. Target buyer: race directors, race series and their sponsors. Pricing to test: $1,500 for one race pilot, then $5,000–$15,000 for a defined annual series. Own the hosted briefing product, while licensing each organizer's edition. This is the best first sales experiment because it connects your sports background to a tightly scoped build.

### 3. Youth sports field arrival guides (N05)

Replace a visiting family's vague field number with a host-verified visual arrival: the right parking/drop-off point, gate, field and meeting spot in a recognizable sports complex. Let the host mark and approve those facts instead of inferring them from open-map doors. A tournament director can reuse the guide across schedules and visiting teams. Target buyer: youth tournament directors, leagues and sports-complex operators. Pricing to test: $750 for a tournament pilot or $1,500–$5,000 annually per complex, with a defined update allowance. Own the common authoring product and license venue editions. Start with one complex that will actively distribute the guide to families.

### 4. Game-day venue experiences (E18)

Build a small pre-game fan journey around a real venue, combining a useful arrival scene with an approved sponsor activity. A team might use one branded quest or a local fan challenge before adding live match data, merchandise or multiplayer. Keep the first deliverable compact enough for one founder to support. Target buyer: team partnership departments, stadium marketers and sponsorship agencies. Pricing to test: $3,000 for one limited activation, then $10,000–$30,000 for an explicitly bounded season package. Retain the general fan experience and tools; license team content and season use. This offers high partnership upside, but custom team approvals can slow the first sale.

### 5. Coach and run-club route briefings (N03)

Give a coach or club leader a way to brief next week's session in a real local scene: where to meet, where to turn, which stretch is the effort segment and where everyone regroups. Annotations come from the coach; the engine supplies a clear shared place rather than making fitness or route-safety decisions. Start with one saved route and an easy briefing export or in-app share. Target buyer: running coaches, clubs and sports retailers hosting community sessions. Pricing to test: $39/month per coach or $299/year per club. Own the workspace and license branded club editions. The key validation is whether a repeat briefing saves enough effort to replace free map links.

### 6. Event location previews (E10)

Sell a simple event arrival guide that shows the host-confirmed approach, entrance and meeting point in the actual neighborhood. The product handles orientation before arrival; the organizer remains responsible for current closures, access and event-specific instructions. Reuse the youth-sports and game-day authoring tools instead of starting a separate business. Target buyer: event organizers, recurring venues and ticketing partners. Pricing to test: $750/event or $149/month per venue with capped events and edits. License the branded presentation and keep the authoring platform. Begin on iPhone with organizer-led distribution; broad browser embeds depend on the planned web version.

### 7. Charity walk fundraising worlds (N02)

Make a charity walk feel like a collective journey through a familiar place, with a clear route, team milestones and sponsor recognition. Keep donations in the charity's existing fundraising system, and let organizers provide approved route information. The first product can record simple opt-in progress without requiring continuous participant GPS. Target buyer: charities, corporate sponsors and fundraising agencies. Pricing to test: $1,500 for a pilot or $5,000 for an annual challenge series, independent of donations. Own the shared challenge tools and license event editions. This can reuse quest and walking components, but validate charity budget and sponsor funding before adding payments or complicated reward mechanics.

### 8. Real-place brand video studio (N47)

Give a sports or local-brand agency a repeatable way to create a short campaign clip in an authentic local setting. Choose an address, approved brand assets, camera path, time of day and season; produce a clearly stylized finished scene for the client's channel. Start as a founder-operated render service to learn which requests repeat before building a self-service studio. Target buyer: creative agencies, sports marketers and local advertisers. Pricing to test: $250 for a bounded render pack or $399/month with explicit output and revision caps. License rendered outputs and later the workspace; retain reusable templates and the engine. Export reliability is the main added engineering dependency.

### 9. DogWell (E01)

Keep developing DogWell as a focused walking routine around an owner's real neighborhood. The world gives a familiar setting for choosing a manageable loop, seeing weather context and remembering completed walks; owners supply their dog's needs and preferences. Concentrate on repeat use and a clear dog-specific reason to return before adding broad wellness, lost-pet coordination or multiple sponsored activities. Target buyer: dog owners, with pet brands as potential pack sponsors. Pricing to test: free starter experience, $4.99/month or $29.99/year, plus optional sponsor-funded content. Keep the app, character, routines and customer relationship exclusive. License general engine capabilities outside its reserved pet-walking product category.

### 10. Outdoor photo and film scouting (N37)

Let a photographer or production scout collect likely camera positions and compare sky/time settings before traveling to a location. The deliverable is a scouting storyboard that supports an on-site check; it should label unverified occluders and avoid promising precise shade from incomplete building or tree geometry. Start with saved viewpoints, sun direction and simple still exports. Target buyer: photographers, production scouts and creative agencies. Pricing to test: $19/month for an individual or $99/month for a team. License the workspace and client outputs. Reuse the camera/export tools built for brand clips, while testing whether a practical preparation workflow converts better than a generic sun planner.

## Keep exclusive versus license

“Exclusive” is a product strategy recommendation concerning your app identity, authored gameplay/content, interface and customer relationship. It does not assert exclusive ownership of open map data or upstream live feeds.

**Keep as owned consumer products:** DogWell and Neighborhood Jobs. Preserve their names, core routines/gameplay, distinctive content and audience. The same applies to a successful consumer quest network or detective/strategy franchise if validated. Sponsor participation and limited branded editions can coexist with retained ownership; do not sell permanent platform-wide exclusivity for one campaign.

**Own the common product; license use or editions:** route briefing, arrival authoring, quests, fan experiences and creative scene outputs. Typical proposed boundaries are a named event, venue, organization, term, usage volume and approved channels. Sponsors may buy limited campaign/category exclusivity tied to place and time. Reserve the engine and general tools for other customers.

**License through specialist partners:** verified accessibility guides, curriculum, newsroom weather presentation, property context, solar proposal scenes, care/logistics/utility workflows and installer/catalog tools. These need evidence, content or operations partners beyond the current engine. The founder should supply the visual/authoring component rather than assume every domain workflow personally.

**Delay broad platform licensing:** the game SDK and address-agent platform. A narrow paid scene/output interface can teach what customers actually need before taking on a generic platform and long-term developer support.

License rendered scenes separately from raw/live data redistribution. Customer ownership of their contributed content, necessary brand permissions, upstream feed display rights, source notices and open-data obligations remain inputs to each actual product agreement. The prior aircraft/transit research can inform feed choices; this brainstorm does not grant any data-use rights.

## Validate the leading opportunities

1. **Race preview:** offer one director a $1,500 limited pilot. Ask for the approved route, a named reviewer and a distribution commitment. The pass condition is a paid brief and access to entrant feedback, not appreciation of the demo.
2. **Quest campaign:** present one agency/brand a five-stop sample with a $2,500 offer. Define the campaign outcome and reporting before building check-in verification or a rewards network.
3. **Sports arrival guide:** demonstrate one verified complex to its operator and ask for a $750 tournament pilot. Observe whether families can identify the correct approach and meeting point in the prototype.
4. **DogWell:** measure repeat walks and test a real paid offer before adding a broad wellness line. Existing usage and revenue, if available, should outweigh these speculative ordinal scores.

Use interviews, paid pilots and observed behavior to revise scores. No delivery timetable, conversion target or revenue forecast has been invented here. The first pilot can use the iPhone product; web embeds, self-serve publishing and multi-tenant account systems are additional work.
